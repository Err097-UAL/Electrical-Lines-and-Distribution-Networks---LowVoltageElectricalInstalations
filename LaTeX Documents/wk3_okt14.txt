\chapter{Radial Network Design and Calculation}

\section{Introduction}
Radial networks are the simplest and most common form of electrical distribution topology. They consist of a single power source from which individual feeder lines branch out to supply loads, resembling the branches of a tree. While cost-effective to build, their primary drawback is a lack of redundancy; a fault on a feeder will cause an outage for all downstream loads.

This section details the analysis of radial networks using the **method of electrical moments**, a simplified yet powerful technique for calculating voltage drop in networks with multiple loads. The analysis covers scenarios with concentrated, distributed, and uniform loads, corresponding to industrial, residential, and street lighting applications.

\section{Methodology: The Method of Electrical Moments}
The method of electrical moments simplifies voltage drop calculations by avoiding complex phasors for resistive lines. The core concept is the **electrical moment ($M$)** of a load, defined as the product of its current ($I$) and its distance ($d$) from a reference point, being a perfect analogy to the mechanical moment
\begin{equation}
    M = I \cdot d
\end{equation}
The total voltage drop ($\Delta U$) at any point in the network is proportional to the sum of the moments of all downstream loads. The formula is adjusted based on the system type (single-phase or three-phase). For a segment of the line, the voltage drop is:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta U_{\text{segment}} = \frac{K}{\sigma \cdot s} \sum (I_{\text{downstream}} \cdot d_{\text{segment}})
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K$ is the phase factor (2 for single-phase, 1 for three-phase line-to-neutral).
    \item $\sigma$ is the conductivity of the conductor material.
    \item $s$ is the conductor's cross-sectional area.
\end{itemize}
The total voltage drop at a node is the cumulative sum of the drops in all preceding segments.

\chapter{Analysis of Networks Fed from Both Ends}

\section{Introduction}
A network fed from both ends, or a dual-fed network, offers significantly higher reliability and improved voltage quality compared to a simple radial line. By supplying a line from two sources, the load is shared, which reduces the current in any single conductor, thereby decreasing voltage drop and power losses.

Crucially, this topology provides redundancy. If one source or a section of the line fails, the network can still be supplied from the other end, preventing a total outage. This section details the analysis of such networks, including the calculation of the current division point, the minimum voltage, and a reliability assessment under failure conditions.

\section{Methodology}
The analysis of a dual-fed network involves determining how the total load current ($I_T$) is split between the two sources, Source A ($I_a$) and Source B ($I_b$).

\subsection{Current Division Calculation}
The current supplied from Source A is calculated using the principle of superposition, considering both the contribution from the loads and the contribution from the voltage difference between the sources:
\begin{equation}
    I_a = \frac{U_a - U_b}{R_{\text{total}}} + \frac{1}{L_{\text{total}}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} I_i (L_{\text{total}} - l_i)
\end{equation}
Where $R_{\text{total}}$ is the total resistance of the line, $L_{\text{total}}$ is the total length, and the summation term represents the moments of each load relative to Source B. The current from Source B is then simply:
\begin{equation}
    I_b = I_T - I_a
\end{equation}

\subsection{Reliability Analysis}
For the "Backup Feeding" scenario (Student C), a reliability analysis is performed. This involves simulating the failure of each source individually. When a source fails, the network ceases to be dual-fed and operates as a simple radial network fed from the remaining source. The voltage profile is recalculated under these radial conditions to assess the impact of the failure and determine if the system remains stable.

\chapter{Ring and Meshed Network Analysis}
\section{Introduction}
Ring networks represent a significant step up in reliability from radial systems. A ring network is essentially a feeder line that loops back to connect to the source from two directions. This closed-loop topology ensures that there are two independent paths of supply to every load on the ring.

The primary advantage is reliability: if there is an open-circuit fault at any point in the ring, no customers lose power. The ring automatically reconfigures into two separate radial feeders. This section details the analysis of a simple ring network by converting it into an equivalent dual-fed line and includes a comprehensive comparison tool to evaluate its performance against a radial alternative.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Equivalent Dual-Fed Model}
A simple ring network with a single feed-in point can be solved by conceptually "opening" the ring at the feed point. This transforms the ring into a linear network of the same total length, which is fed from both ends by the same voltage source. Once converted, the exact same calculation methods used for dual-fed networks can be applied to find the clockwise and anti-clockwise current distribution, and to locate the minimum voltage point.

\subsection{Network Comparison Tool}
To provide a holistic design perspective, a comparison tool was developed. This tool analyzes a ring network and an equivalent radial network serving the identical set of loads, comparing them across four key metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Voltage Quality:} Compares the maximum voltage drop in each topology under normal operation.
    \item \textbf{Reliability:} Simulates a single open-circuit fault and analyzes the impact on voltage stability. For a radial network, this results in a partial outage, whereas the ring maintains supply.
    \item \textbf{Economic Analysis:} Calculates and compares the total lifecycle cost, including the initial cable cost and the cost of energy losses over a 20-year period.
    \item \textbf{Schematic and Loading Diagrams:} Provides side-by-side visual comparisons of the network layouts and current distributions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results}
The comparison tool was run for a sample set of loads. The results are presented in a series of plots generated by the tool, showcasing the trade-offs between the two topologies. The loading diagram comparison (Figure \ref{fig:loading_comp}) clearly shows how the ring network reduces the maximum current in any conductor segment.

\chapter{List of Formulas}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Electrical Moment:} $M = I \cdot L$
    \item \textbf{Radial Voltage Drop (Segment):} $\Delta U_{\text{seg}} = \frac{K}{\sigma \cdot s} \cdot I_{\text{seg}} \cdot L_{\text{seg}}$
    \item \textbf{Current from Source A (Dual-Fed):} $I_a = \frac{U_a - U_b}{R_{\text{total}}} + \frac{1}{L_{\text{total}}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} I_i (L_{\text{total}} - l_i)$
    \item \textbf{Current from Source B (Dual-Fed):} $I_b = (\sum I_i) - I_a$
\end{enumerate}
